## Semantic Reorganization of the Site.

Code speaks. And it tells Google what your page is about.

But often, the message is confusing. Mislabelled sections, broken hierarchies, important content hidden under generic tags.

Reorganizing semantics is not about changing the design. It’s about making the HTML structure tell the same story you want to communicate.

---

### Why it happens

When we build a website quickly, we tend to prioritize visuals. Making it look good. But the invisible part—the code—remains cluttered.

- `divs` are used for everything, without considering `header`, `main`, `article`, or `aside`.
- Headings jump from `H2` to `H5` without logical order.
- Sections are not marked as such.
- Relationships between pages are lost due to poor link architecture.

Google reads code, not design. If the code doesn't communicate well, the page loses value.



---

### What it is

Semantic reorganization means cleaning and ordering the website's skeleton.

- I review HTML5 tags so that each block has the correct meaning.
- I order the heading hierarchy so it makes sense from top to bottom.
- I correctly mark articles, navigation, and footers.
- I review internal links to connect related content.

It’s not visible at first glance, but when you do it, Google understands better what you’re talking about and what is important.

---

### What it affects

Poor semantics confuse search engines. And that has consequences:

- **Featured Snippets**: if you don't mark a list or a paragraph well, Google won't show it as a featured result.
- **Topic Comprehension**: without a clear hierarchy, the engine struggles to identify the page's main topic.
- **Accessibility**: screen readers also depend on semantics to guide visually impaired users.
- **User Experience**: a cluttered structure is noticeable in navigation.

The damage is silent, but it drags down positioning in the long run.

---

### Possible solutions

When I perform a semantic audit at Alsnippets, I follow a routine:

1. **Heading structure review**. I check that there is only one H1 and that subsequent tags maintain a logical order.
2. **Section tag analysis**. I replace generic `divs` with `section`, `article`, `aside`, or `nav` where appropriate.
3. **Internal navigation improvement**. I add contextual links between related content to strengthen the site's architecture.
4. **Elimination of redundant content**. Sometimes there are blocks that add no value and only generate semantic noise.
5. **Testing with tools**. I use validators to see how Google interprets each page before and after the changes.

The result is a website that is easier for machines to read and clearer for people.

This work usually goes hand in hand with a good [intent and structure analysis](/en/analisis-intencion-estructura).

---

### Tips

- **Think of a table of contents, not design**. When layouting, ask yourself: "if this were a book, what chapter label would it have?".
- **Don't abuse the H1**. One per page is enough. More than one causes confusion.
- **Use Chrome's Inspector**. Look at the code as if you were Google. Do you see clearly what is important?
